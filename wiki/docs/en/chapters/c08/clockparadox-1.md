<p>The spaceship steps into the depth of the universe, as its engine rumbles. The mining machines wander among the asteroid belt off the racetrack. Viewing far out to the south pole of Saturn, there is a giant clock face, so profound as the eye of the white whole. The spaceship of the first men, Ark I, stops here, in the still time, falling back occasionally as a mirage.</p>
<p>From the distance to the near, countless needles with unknown scales rotates slowly, clockwise or anticlockwise. Some stays in silence for years without moving for one second, while others move as swiftly as photons, spin and sink from the highest point, falling into the far depth of the inner core.</p>
<p>Those needles are nearly identical, all connected in series by the pole, maintaining the clockwork in an incomprehensible way. Perhaps the only clock needle we see has always been that same one, while the others are mere fading phantoms, filling in the gaps of the real numbers, and then become nameless new real number scales, until no longer divisible.</p>
<p>The seemingly never-stopping time appears like a view of moving frames. It stays still at its smallest scale, yet relative to the observers, the stillness obtains the nature of movement. Whether it is due to the movement or the stillness of the observer, we are yet to know.</p>
<p>This is the clock paradox.</p>
